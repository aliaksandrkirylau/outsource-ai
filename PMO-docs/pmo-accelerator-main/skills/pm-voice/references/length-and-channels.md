# Length and channels

Read this before drafting anything a PM will send. Length is decided first.

## Where these numbers come from

Two sources, and they are not the same kind of evidence.

- **The shape of each channel** - how many sentences, whether there are paragraphs, when structure
  appears - comes from reading Innowise PMO Google Chat spaces (division heads, RMs, PMO, PMs) in
  September 2026. It describes the house, by channel and role, and it is the part to trust.
- **The word ceilings** were calibrated on one PM's measured writing (median 25 words English, 32
  Russian, half of all messages under 30). They are a starting point, not a house measurement. The
  house runs SHORTER than that PM: most messages in these channels are a single line.

If the PM has saved writing samples, their own lengths beat the ceilings below.

## Budgets by channel

| Channel | Shape | Starting ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| DM reply, chat reply, acknowledgement | 1-2 sentences, often a fragment | 25 words |
| Answer to a direct question, any channel | 1 short paragraph | 60 words |
| Team-chat instruction or ask | 1-3 sentences | 50 words |
| Client chat reply | 1-3 sentences | 50 words |
| Post to the PM group or a peer channel | 1-2 short paragraphs, or a numbered ask | 120 words |
| Client email | 2-4 short paragraphs. Two sentences is a complete email | 200 words |
| Weekly status to leadership | the required labels, one short paragraph or line each | 400 words |
| Release or build post to a client | one caveat line, one line per ticket, one closing line | no fixed cap - prose only where it earns it |

Anything over its ceiling needs a reason that survives being said out loud. "It was a complicated
week" is not one.

**Match the channel's length, not the topic's importance.** A one-line question in a DM gets a
one-line answer even when the answer is consequential. A composed three-paragraph reply to a
one-line "what is the status on X?" is the wrong shape however good the content is.

## How to cut

1. **Delete the first sentence.** It is almost always scene-setting the reader already has.
2. **Delete the last sentence.** It is almost always a recap or an offer.
3. **Collapse "X, which means Y" into Y.** State the conclusion; the reader infers the mechanism.
4. **One hedge per uncertain claim, not three.** Hedging is human. Stacking hedges is not.
5. **Cut ceremony, never specificity.** Names, ticket IDs, dates and numbers stay. The framing around
   them goes.
6. **A question does not need a preamble.** Open with the question; context follows if needed.
7. **A condition goes inside the sentence**, not in a caveat paragraph after a clean promise: "We
   will look at it, but after the priority items and only if there is time."

**The test:** would a PM have typed this between two calls? If it reads as composed, it is too long.

## How the channels read

Patterns by channel and role, not by person, and not a judgement of anyone's writing.

**Everywhere**
- Most messages are one line. A structured multi-paragraph post is a weekly or monthly status, an
  escalation, or a broadcast to the whole group. Everything else is a sentence or two.
- Emoji reactions carry most acknowledgements. A short message often gets a reaction, not a reply.
- Numbered points `1) 2) 3)` when a message has structure. Markdown bullets are rare.
- Bold appears in bot output and formal broadcasts, almost nowhere else. A bolded label in a chat
  message looks machine-made.
- Typos stay, at every level of seniority. Nobody re-sends to fix one.
- Abbreviation without ceremony is normal, and `->` works as an inline arrow.
- Nobody signs off in chat. A greeting opens the first message of a thread and nothing else.

**Division head or lead to a PM, in a DM** - telegraphic. One clause per message, often several
short messages in a row rather than one composed one. The reply that fits is short and immediate.

**PMO broadcast to the PM group** - a one-line greeting, the context in one sentence, the asks as
numbered points, an explicit CC line, the link at the end.

**Escalation about a person or a problem** - numbered evidence, then a factual close saying what the
situation is now. No softening preamble and no HR language. When one item is weaker than the
others, say so on that item ("subjective point - ...") rather than dressing it up to match.

**Status from a PM** - a label, then prose. Close on a label that names what happens next
("Strategy:", "Next:"). Lowercase is fine mid-message.

**Client chat** - the primary client channel for most projects. Short, direct, specific. The
version, the date, what the client has to do. "Let me know if..." is fine with an object ("...if you
still see the OTP error") and a tell without one ("...if you'd like me to adjust anything").

**Client email** - a greeting line, the point in the first paragraph, what happens next in the last.
Two sentences is a complete email. Close on substance, not "Looking forward to hearing from you".

## Templated and generated status posts

Some recurring status posts in PMO channels are produced by a bot or filled from a template. A bot
post is obvious. The in-between case - a status that was drafted with heavy assistance - tends to
show all of these at once, where the same author's ordinary chat shows none:

- zero typos in a channel where typos are universal
- no hedging words anywhere
- every template section filled, including a dash for "nothing to say" where a person writes "none"
  or drops the line
- every section built as the same complete sentence shape
- full dates (`14.09.2026`) and full formal names where the channel writes `14.09`, "Friday", a short
  name or an @mention

None of that is proof, and a careful person writing to a template does some of it. It is a list of
things NOT to produce. A PM's own status in the same genre keeps its hedges, keeps its typos and
writes "none" for an empty section - the three things a generated draft strips out first.

**Do not out-polish the channel.**
